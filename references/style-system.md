# Painterly Contrast Visual System

Use this reference to design the image. Treat numeric ranges as starting targets, not rigid measurements.

## Fixed System

These rules survived cross-season, bright/dark, close/wide, character/environment, action/quiet, and FX comparisons.

### 1. One perceptually dominant event

Place one subject, relationship, gesture, object, or light event inside the middle attention zone—roughly the central 40–60% of the canvas. It may be off-center or very small. Make it the first read through isolation, local value contrast, chroma, edge sharpness, scale, perspective, or a combination.

Do not mechanically center every subject. “Center-emphasized” means perceptual dominance near the middle, not passport-photo symmetry.

Before surface design, write the dramatic proposition and directorial transform proposal from [directorial-contrast.md](directorial-contrast.md). In Style-first/Expressive work, one primary macro departure must change the composition decision at thumbnail scale; attractive brushwork is not a substitute.

### 2. Three large value groups

Start near a 60/30/10 distribution:

- dominant quiet field;
- supporting structural mass;
- small light/dark apex.

Merge clutter into one of these groups. Reserve the brightest light and deepest dark for the focal path. A dark frame must retain readable shadow separation; a high-key frame must retain at least one anchoring dark mass.

Select the exposure key from the scene. High-key mist, daylight, snow, pale interiors, and luminous organic spaces are fully valid. Do not lower the whole exposure merely to signal this visual family; create contrast through grouped values, hue separation, shaped shadow, and local edge control.

Distribute contrast by saliency tier rather than applying one global curve. Let the focal owner peak in two or three useful dimensions; let supporting structure retain medium cues; compress context local contrast, microcontrast, hard-edge density, hue noise, and texture frequency into a scene-owned colored field. Preserve broad depth and material cues.

### 3. Scene-owned color contrast

Audit the source/story and choose a color-authorship mode from [color-authorship.md](color-authorship.md) before naming hues. Assign colors by function and spatial owner, not franchise habit:

- **Dominant field:** atmosphere and emotional baseline;
- **Structural counter:** separates subject from environment or near from far;
- **Focal accent:** marks the story event, usually a small area;
- **Optional memory/echo:** repeats once to connect distant regions.
- **Neutral bridge:** prevents all regions from competing and keeps materials readable.

Keep the highest chroma controlled and give it clear attention ownership; its area may be small or may belong to a larger focal subject. Tint shadows deliberately rather than defaulting to neutral black or one universal shadow hue.

Choose one primary contrast axis—value, analogous harmony, complementary/split-complementary, warm-cool, saturation, hue-boundary, near-monochrome rupture, or local color versus colored illumination—and at most one subordinate axis. Warm-cool is optional; near-monochrome, analogous, or value-led solutions can be equally specific. Do not default to teal-orange, purple-neon, blue hair, acid green, or any palette sampled from one published frame.

The generated frame owns its color outcome: preserve an already functional source palette, rebalance a weak hierarchy, or re-script when allowed and justified. A global grade, decorative accent, or list of attractive hues is not color authorship.

### 4. Motivated, shape-readable light

Choose one primary source that can be inferred from the image: window, door, fire, lamp, portal, screen, sky opening, reflected ground, or supernatural core. Give the source a visible geometry. Add at most one subordinate bounce or rim.

Use haze to reveal selected beams, not to fog the whole frame. Keep bloom tight. Do not outline every dark surface with colored rim light.

### 5. Spatial depth plus graphic surface

Build believable depth with perspective, overlap, foreground occlusion, scale falloff, and atmospheric separation. On top of that space, use hand-painted planar color breaks and occasional flat graphic FX.

The result should feel like designed animation made spatial, not a photograph or smooth 3D render with an oil-paint filter.

### 6. Reconstruct shapes before marks

For photo remakes, do not preserve photographic shading and then cover it with texture. First translate the image into designed shapes:

- simplify the outer silhouette into a few intentional directional breaks;
- replace continuous facial/object gradients with 4–7 meaningful planes;
- merge folds, leaves, water droplets, rubble, windows, or repeated props into larger rhythm groups;
- let one or two asymmetric contour accents carry character;
- preserve recognition anchors, not incidental realism.

If the frame still reads as the original photograph beneath one uniform brush treatment, the transformation failed.

### 7. Environment as a designed subject

When the environment carries the focal event, do not fall back to realistic scenic illustration. Give one landform, structure, void, weather mass, or light shape the role normally carried by a character silhouette. Oppose it with one source-owned current or counterform such as a river, road, forest wall, shadow wedge, cloud band, machinery line, or crowd flow.

In Style-first/Expressive work, compress repeated assets into a few rhythm groups and make one primary macro departure plus one supporting move—scale takeover, area reallocation, crop/restage, foreground occlusion, perspective/depth compression, negative-space takeover, light-shape rearchitecture, color-zone restaging, or a motivated subjective rupture. Preserve declared anchors, but let the frame become more designed than documented. The primary move must change a major mass, contour, overlap, focal scale, light topology, or color-zone relationship at 128–256 px. A beautiful frame without visible transfiguration is still too literal for this mode.

Use [environment-abstraction.md](environment-abstraction.md) for the full contract. Do not apply its distortion rules to protected faces, products, text, architecture, maps, or documentary geometry.

### 8. Selective edge and detail hierarchy

- Hardest edges: eyes, expression, key silhouette break, weapon/gesture tip, portal seam, or story-critical prop.
- Medium edges: focal clothing, nearby machinery, important architecture.
- Soft/lost edges: haze, hair merging into shadow, distant forms, peripheral wall/floor, fast motion.

Increase detail toward the focal event and foreground. Compress distant buildings, foliage, crowds, and repeated machinery into broad masses.

Do not equate context compression with uniform softness. Keep a few structural edges, perspective cues, and material boundaries; remove equal competition rather than all information.

### 9. Faceted painterly modeling

Model faces and objects with stepped planes rather than uniform gradients. For a face, 4–7 meaningful plane changes are usually enough before small accents. Allow cool and warm shifts within skin, but keep skin quieter than scar, fabric, wall, metal, smoke, or magical material.

Use visible brush boundaries locally. Avoid global canvas grain, noisy pores, waxy subsurface skin, wet-plastic highlights, or evenly sharp hair strands.

Assign material-specific shape and mark grammar rather than one universal paint texture. The following are examples, not a required material list; select only relevant rows and derive unlisted materials from scale, rigidity, reflectance, motion, and focal importance.

| Material | Primary construction | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Skin | quiet warm/cool planes; sparse sharp accents at eyes, nose, mouth | pores, stippling, impasto everywhere |
| Hair | grouped wedges and broken silhouette clumps | individually rendered strands |
| Cloth/rainwear | angular fold planes, a few graphic seams, controlled highlights | tracing every wrinkle or wet specular |
| Water | broad directional ribbons, flat white breaks, soft mist gaps | equal short strokes across the entire waterfall |
| Rock/architecture | large interlocking facets with selective cracks | uniform pebble/noise texture |
| Foliage | clustered masses plus a few sharp foreground blades | leaf-by-leaf replication |
| Metal | crisp planar accents and limited reflected streaks | full-length glossy edges on every part |

### 10. Graphic 2D intervention and FX

Scenes without narrative FX can still carry a graphic layer through a few flat seams, contour fragments, directional dry-brush lines, simplified motion/atmosphere shapes, or deliberately broken color boundaries. Choose marks from the subject rather than adding magical effects by default.

Give every important effect a distinct shape language and color role: technical circle, fractured ribbon, vapor plume, flat line drawing, particulate core, membrane, shard burst, or another scene-specific grammar.

Within one effect, vary edges: one sharp core or seam, broader motion ribbons, sparse dust, and controlled halo. Do not use the same glowing particles for every power.

### 11. Readable verb before spectacle

For action, choose one verb—strike, flee, brace, reach, fall, aim, confront—and make the silhouette/pose communicate it without particles. Use limbs, weapons, chains, cloth, camera angle, and environmental diagonals as directional vectors. Add debris only as punctuation.

## Multi-scale Design

### Macro: 3-second thumbnail

- Can the image be reduced to three value masses?
- Is the focal event the first read?
- Is there a large quiet or occluding region?
- Does the viewing path end at the intended subject?
- For environment-emphasis, do a hero form and counterforce remain legible after repeated detail disappears?
- For a source remake, does the source/result blur map show a different major area, contour, overlap, focal scale, light shape, or color-zone adjacency rather than only different texture?
- Do non-focal regions read first as one or two chromatic-gray/scene-owned fields while retaining depth?

### Meso: subject and environment

- Use one composition family.
- Separate subject and environment through value, hue, edge, or atmosphere—not all four at maximum.
- Let props and architecture explain the world without duplicating the subject's contrast.

### Micro: face, material, and mark

- Concentrate microcontrast around eyes/expression or one narrative mark.
- Differentiate skin, cloth, metal, wall, smoke, and energy through plane size, edge, and reflectance.
- Keep peripheral texture broad and incomplete.

## Composition Families

Choose one primary family:

1. **Aperture frame:** dark doorway, arch, foreground body, machinery, or foliage encloses a brighter central island.
2. **Environment canyon:** side masses and perspective rays lead to a central tower, figure, vehicle, or threshold.
3. **Isolated void:** a small subject/light event sits in 70–90% quiet atmosphere.
4. **Opposed profiles:** two faces/bodies form a compressed diagonal or negative-space wedge.
5. **Diagonal action:** pose and environment share one directional thrust; keep the opposite corner quiet.
6. **Circular engine:** ring, wheel, halo, or machine centers attention while figures offset the symmetry.
7. **Threshold stage:** a hard pool of light, door rectangle, or floor plane separates exposed subject from shadow audience.
8. **Monument and current:** one enlarged landform/structure/void dominates while a river, road, crowd, shadow, or weather ribbon pulls toward or against it.
9. **Ruptured strata:** broad stacked sky/land/water/interior bands are interrupted by one vertical, diagonal, void, or light break.
10. **Extreme fragment:** one source-owned face, hand, prop, beam, branch, wall, or machine part crosses the frame as an enlarged foreground shape while the main subject/environment becomes a counterform.

Use canted framing only when instability, intoxication, danger, or broken order benefits the story.

## Variable Recipes

Change these according to source and story:

- darkness level and exposure key;
- exact hues and saturation area;
- color-authorship mode, primary contrast axis, and neutral balance;
- stable, low/high, or canted camera;
- close, medium, or wide shot;
- subject size and centrality;
- haze density and beam visibility;
- surface wear, cleanliness, and ornament;
- FX shape language;
- brush coarseness and motion abstraction.

## Optional World Tendencies

Use only when the user's world calls for them.

- **Ordered civic/technical:** stable axes, clean daylight, geometric ornament, cream/brass/blue separation, controlled sky, maintained surfaces.
- **Layered industrial/undercity:** oblique framing, nested arches/pipes, steam occlusion, worn dark surfaces, sparse toxic/neon lines, compressed headroom.
- **Organic transcendent:** pale connected membranes, garden greens, coral/pink seams, luminous voids, small dark human anchors.
- **Ruptured psychic:** near-black field, red or white tears, distorted scale, graphic strands, minimal literal architecture.

## Anti-identity and Sample Residue

Exclude by default:

- known franchise characters, hairstyles, costumes, tattoos, weapons, logos, runes, city designs, or exact frame layouts;
- blue-haired/pink-haired “rebels” as an automatic shortcut;
- hexagonal/rune motifs unless the user's subject independently requires them;
- generic anime cel shading, uniform comic outlines, chibi proportions, superhero poster symmetry;
- photoreal skin inside painterly backgrounds;
- uniform neon, excessive bloom, all-over scratches, random sparks, wet-gloss materials, and muddy crushed blacks.
